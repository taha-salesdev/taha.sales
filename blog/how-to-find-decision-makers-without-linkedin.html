How to Find Decision Makers When Their Contact Information Isn't Public

By Taha Bin Ishfaq  |  August 30, 2026  |  5 min read

Most prospecting guides assume your target buyer has a fully updated LinkedIn profile and exists in Apollo or ZoomInfo. But in industries like manufacturing, construction, logistics, and traditional finance, decision makers are often invisible online. When standard tools fail, you have to dig deeper.

Step 1: The "About Us" and Team Pages

The simplest method is often the most overlooked. Small to mid-sized traditional businesses frequently list their leadership team on their website. Check the "About Us," "Our Team," or "Leadership" pages. Even if they don't provide emails, getting the specific name and title is the critical first step.

Step 2: Press Releases and Media Kits

Search Google for the company name alongside terms like "announces," "press release," or "appoints." When a company hires a new executive, launches a product, or attends a trade show, they issue press releases. These almost always include quotes from decision makers (giving you their name) and contact info for a media rep (giving you the company's email pattern).

Step 3: State Business Filings

In the US, every registered business must file annual reports with the Secretary of State where they operate. These filings are public record and mandate listing the principal officers (President, CEO, Directors). Search the relevant state's business entity database to find the legal names of the owners.

Step 4: Guessing and Verifying the Email

Once you have the name from one of the methods above, you need the email. Use Hunter.io to find the company's email pattern (e.g., firstinitial.lastname@domain.com). Apply that pattern to the executive's name, and run the resulting email through a verifier like NeverBounce. If it comes back "Valid," you've found a direct line to an unlisted executive.

Step 5: The Top-Down Approach (Calling)

If you cannot find the specific decision maker's name, call the main company line and take a top-down approach. Ask for the office of the President or CEO. When the assistant answers, be honest: "I'm trying to reach the person who handles [X]. Could you point me in the right direction?" Assistants are gatekeepers, but they will often route you correctly if you are direct and professional.
